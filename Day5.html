📈 1. Correlation

🔬 Research Question: Does the amount of study time relate to examination score?

When do we use correlation?

Correlation is generally used when we want to study the linear relationship between two numerical variables.

VariableType
Study HoursNumerical
Examination ScoreNumerical

Correlation coefficient (r)

  • r = +1 → Perfect positive relationship
  • r = 0 → No linear relationship
  • r = −1 → Perfect negative relationship
Important: Correlation measures association, not causation.

🔬 Interactive Correlation Demonstration

Select a relationship and observe the scatterplot.

💻 R Code: Correlation

data <- data.frame(
  Study_Hours = c(2,3,4,5,6,7,8,4,6,9),
  Score = c(45,50,55,65,70,72,80,60,68,90)
)

# Scatterplot
plot(data$Study_Hours, data$Score,
     main = "Study Hours vs Exam Score",
     xlab = "Study Hours",
     ylab = "Exam Score")

# Correlation coefficient
cor(data$Study_Hours, data$Score)

# Correlation hypothesis test
cor.test(data$Study_Hours, data$Score)

How to Report

There was a positive relationship between study hours and examination score (r = ..., p = ...).

📉 2. Simple Linear Regression

🔬 Research Question: Can examination score be predicted from study hours?

Correlation asks

Are X and Y related?

Regression asks

How does Y change when X changes?

Regression Model

Ŷ = b₀ + b₁X
SymbolMeaning
ŶPredicted dependent variable
b₀Intercept
b₁Regression coefficient (slope)
XIndependent variable

📊 Interactive Regression Example


💻 R Code: Simple Linear Regression

model <- lm(Score ~ Study_Hours, data = data)

summary(model)

# Scatterplot
plot(data$Study_Hours, data$Score,
     xlab = "Study Hours",
     ylab = "Exam Score")

# Add regression line
abline(model, col = "red", lwd = 2)

Interpretation

If the coefficient for Study_Hours is 5.2:

For every one-hour increase in study time, examination score is expected to increase by approximately 5.2 points.

Understanding R²

R² tells us how much variation in the dependent variable is explained by the model.

If R² = 0.72:
Approximately 72% of the variation in examination scores is explained by study hours.

data2 <- data.frame(
  Performance = c(60,65,70,75,80,85,90,72,68,88),

model2 <- lm(
  Performance ~ Experience + Training + Satisfaction,
  data = data2
)

summary(model2)

👥 4. Relationship Between Qualitative Variables

🔬 Research Question: Is gender associated with preference for online teaching?
Variable Type
Gender Qualitative / Categorical
Teaching Preference Qualitative / Categorical

👉 Appropriate Statistical Methods

Chi-square Test
Used to test whether two categorical variables are statistically associated.
Fisher's Exact Test
Preferred for a 2 × 2 table when expected cell frequencies are small, particularly when an expected frequency is less than 5.
Odds Ratio (OR)
Measures the strength and direction of association between two categorical variables in a 2 × 2 table.

📊 Example 2 × 2 Contingency Table

Gender Prefer Online Do Not Prefer Total
Male 40 20 60
Female 55 15 70
Total 95 35 130

This is a 2 × 2 contingency table because there are two categories of gender and two categories of teaching preference.

🧪 Hypotheses

H₀:
Gender and teaching preference are independent.
H₁:
Gender and teaching preference are associated.

📐 1. Chi-square Test of Independence

The Chi-square test of independence is used to determine whether there is a statistically significant association between two categorical variables.

When should we use it?

  • Both variables are categorical.
  • The observations are independent.
  • Expected cell frequencies are sufficiently large.

Expected Frequency

The expected frequency for each cell is calculated as:

Expected Frequency = (Row Total × Column Total) / Grand Total

Decision Rule

If p < 0.05, reject H₀ and conclude that there is a statistically significant association between the two categorical variables.

🎯 2. Fisher's Exact Test

Fisher's exact test is an alternative to the Chi-square test for a 2 × 2 contingency table, particularly when the sample size is small or expected cell frequencies are low.

When should Fisher's Exact Test be considered?

  • The table is 2 × 2.
  • The sample size is small.
  • One or more expected cell frequencies are small.
  • Especially when an expected frequency is less than 5.

Important

Fisher's exact test does not rely on the large-sample approximation used by the Chi-square test. It calculates the exact probability of obtaining the observed table and tables more extreme than it.

📈 3. Odds Ratio (OR)

The odds ratio (OR) measures the strength of association between two categorical variables in a 2 × 2 contingency table.

2 × 2 Table for Odds Ratio

Prefer Online Do Not Prefer
Male a = 40 b = 20
Female c = 55 d = 15

Formula

OR = (a × d) / (b × c)

OR = (40 × 15) / (20 × 55)

OR = 600 / 1100

OR ≈ 0.55

Interpretation of OR

OR Interpretation
OR = 1 No association
OR > 1 Higher odds of the outcome in the first group
OR < 1 Lower odds of the outcome in the first group

In this example, the OR is approximately 0.55. Thus, the odds of preferring online teaching among males are approximately 0.55 times the odds among females, based on the way the table is arranged.

Important: Odds ratio measures association; it does not establish causation.

💻 R Code: Chi-square, Fisher's Exact Test and Odds Ratio

# Create the 2 × 2 contingency table

tab <- matrix(
  c(40, 20,
    55, 15),
  nrow = 2,
  byrow = TRUE
)

# Add row and column names

rownames(tab) <- c("Male", "Female")
colnames(tab) <- c("Prefer_Online", "Do_Not_Prefer")

# Display the table

tab

# ------------------------------------------------
# 1. Chi-square Test
# ------------------------------------------------

chisq.test(tab)

# Check expected frequencies

chisq.test(tab)$expected

# ------------------------------------------------
# 2. Fisher's Exact Test
# ------------------------------------------------

fisher.test(tab)

# ------------------------------------------------
# 3. Odds Ratio
# ------------------------------------------------

# Calculate OR manually

OR <- (40 * 15) / (20 * 55)

OR

# Fisher's exact test also provides an
# odds ratio and confidence interval

fisher.test(tab)$estimate
fisher.test(tab)$conf.int

🔎 Chi-square vs Fisher's Exact Test

Feature Chi-square Test Fisher's Exact Test
Variable type Categorical Categorical
Typical table 2 × 2 or larger Primarily 2 × 2
Large sample Suitable Suitable, but may be computationally unnecessary
Small expected frequencies May be inappropriate Preferred
Provides p-value Yes Yes
Measures strength of association Not directly OR can be reported for 2 × 2 tables

🧭 Which Method Should I Use?

2 categorical variables

↓

Check expected frequencies

↓

Expected frequencies adequate → Chi-square test

Small expected frequencies in a 2 × 2 table → Fisher's exact test

Want the strength of association?

↓

Report Odds Ratio

Preferably with its 95% confidence interval.

📌 Interpretation

If p < 0.05

There is a statistically significant association between gender and teaching preference.

If p ≥ 0.05

There is insufficient evidence to conclude that gender and teaching preference are statistically associated.

📝 Research Reporting Example

A Chi-square test of independence was conducted to examine the association between gender and teaching preference. The results indicated that the association was statistically significant/non-significant, χ²(df) = ..., p = .... The odds ratio was ... (95% CI: ...–...), indicating that the odds of preferring online teaching were ... times higher/lower among males compared with females.

Do not say: Gender causes teaching preference.

Correct: Gender is statistically associated with teaching preference.

⚠️ Important Statistical Note

The Chi-square test or Fisher's exact test tells us whether there is evidence of an association. The odds ratio tells us about the strength and direction of that association.

Therefore, for a 2 × 2 categorical analysis, it is often useful to report both the p-value and the odds ratio with its 95% confidence interval.

Quick Statistical Decision Table

Research SituationRecommended Method
Compare two groups on a numerical outcomeIndependent t-test
Compare three or more groups on a numerical outcomeANOVA

📝 Final Checklist Before Choosing a Test

  1. What is my research question?
  2. What is my dependent/outcome variable?
  3. What are my independent variables?
  4. Are the variables numerical or qualitative?
  5. Am I comparing groups or studying relationships?
  6. What assumptions should I check?
  7. How will I interpret the results?
